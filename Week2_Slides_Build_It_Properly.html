Rule OneMeasure. Never assume.

Catalogue numbers are nominal — a name, not a measurement.

  • A "210 ℓ" drum often holds 200 ℓ
  • "50 × 76 mm" timber measures 44 × 69 mm once planed
  • "4 inch" pipe is 110 mm outside, not 101.6 mm
  • A foam block cut on site is never exactly the size you ordered

None of these are anyone's fault. They are simply how materials are sold. Your job is to check.

What a 10 ℓ error costs Per drum: 10 ℓ = 10 kg lift lost Six drums: 60 kg lost = 4.8% of planned lift, gone
And timber is worse Planed 50 × 76 timber has 20% less cross-section than nominal — weaker than you designed, though happily also lighter.

Hand out tape measures and the scale now. Every patrol measures and weighs their own materials before anything is cut or lashed. This is Section 1 of tonight's homework — they should be recording as they go.

MethodHow to verify a material properly

For every buoyancy item

  1. Measure all three dimensions with a tape — twice, by two people
  2. Weigh it on the scale, empty and dry
  3. Calculate its volume from your measurements, not the label
  4. Record both numbers in your build sheet
Build sheet — one row per item Item · L × W × H · Volume ℓ Mass kg · Qty · Total ℓ · Total kg
Two people, alwaysOne reads the tape, one writes it down. A single person measuring and remembering is how a 3 cm error reaches the water.
Round the safe wayRound volume down and weight up. Errors then make you safer, never riskier.

Part Two

Weight and balance

Week 2 · Construction
07
Transition. We've fixed how much lift. Now: where the weight sits.

BalanceFinding the centre of gravity

The centre of gravity is the single point the whole raft's weight acts through. Find it by taking moments — each mass multiplied by its distance from one end.

The formula CoG = Σ(m × x) ÷ Σm
m = each mass   x = its distance from the stern
Why it matters The centre of buoyancy sits at the middle of your raft's volume. If the centre of gravity isn't above it, the raft tilts until the two line up — bow down, or stern down.
Week 2 · Construction
08
Moments are new maths for some of them. Anchor it with a see-saw: a heavy person close to the middle balances a light person far out. Same equation.

Worked ExampleWhere your crew stands changes everything

All four crowded at the bow Raft 171 kg @ 1.5 m → 256.5 Crew 4 × 80 kg @ 2.2–2.8 m → 800 Σ(m×x) = 1 056.5   Σm = 491 kg CoG = 2.15 m   (0.65 m off centre) bow digs in · freeboard gone at the front
Spread evenly along the deck Raft 171 kg @ 1.5 m → 256.5 Crew 4 × 80 kg @ 0.6–2.4 m → 480 Σ(m×x) = 736.5   Σm = 491 kg CoG = 1.50 m   (dead centre) raft sits level · full freeboard everywhere
Same raft · same total weight · same buoyancy Only the positions changed — and one of these two rafts takes water over the bow.
Week 2 · Construction
09
The point: total load was safe in both cases. Distribution, not weight, sank it. Tell them their crew positions are part of the design and belong on the sketch.

Design RulesThree rules for a stable raft

Rule 1Low

Keep every heavy thing as close to the deck as possible. Crew sit or kneel — never stand. A standing scout raises the centre of gravity by half a metre.

Rule 2Wide

Buoyancy at the outer edges, not bunched in the middle. The further apart your floats, the harder the raft is to tip.

Rule 3Even

Balanced front to back and side to side. Check both. Most patrols remember one and forget the other.

Week 2 · Construction
10
Low, wide, even. Make them repeat it. These three words will save more rafts in Week 3 than any calculation.

The FrameA rectangle is not a structure

One brace turns a mechanism into a structure.

Four poles lashed at four corners will fold flat sideways under load. It isn't weak rope — a rectangle has no fixed shape.

Add a diagonalA triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side. That's the whole reason bridges, pylons and roof trusses are full of triangles.
On your raftAt minimum: one diagonal in each bay, or a full plank deck screwed across the frame, which does the same job.
Week 2 · Construction
12
Demo: four rulers and split pins, or just four poles held loosely. Push one corner — it racks. Add a fifth pole across and it locks. Ten seconds, never forgotten.

JointsThe right lashing for the job

LashingUse it whenWhat it resists
SquareTwo poles cross at 90°Poles sliding along each other
DiagonalTwo poles cross at an angle and want to spring apartRacking — the frame folding sideways
RoundTwo parallel poles joined to make one longer oneThe join bending or pulling apart
ShearTwo poles that open into an A-frameThe legs splaying out
Frapping turns are not optionalThe wrapping turns hold the poles together; the frapping turns across them are what actually tighten the joint. Skip them and the lashing is decoration.
Rope stretches when wetNatural-fibre rope goes slack after its first soaking. Plan to re-tighten every lashing after the raft's first few minutes in the water.

13
Scoutcraft they already have. Connect it to the structural reason: diagonal lashing exists precisely because of slide 12. Most scouts learn the knot without ever being told why.

The Real Failure PointBuoyancy doesn't fail. The attachment does.

A sealed drum will hold up 195 kg all day. The strap holding it to your frame will not.

Under load, the raft presses down and the floats press up. Everything in between is being pulled apart — and a single strap over a round drum is exactly the wrong way to resist that.

DoBuild a cradle the float sits inside. Two straps minimum, crossed. Notch the frame so a round float cannot roll.
Don'tOne ratchet strap over the top. Foam compresses under tension and slides straight out from under it.
Test before waterLift the raft by its frame. If a float shifts even slightly, it will be gone within a minute of launching.
Week 2 · Construction
14
Most common Week 3 failure by a distance. Say it plainly: no patrol has ever lost a raft because the drum stopped floating. They lose it because the drum left.

Air Is The CargoSealing, and how to prove it

Where air escapes

  • Drum bungs without a washer
  • Pipe end caps pushed on, not glued
  • A screw driven through a float to fix it down
  • Cracks from dragging floats over gravel
The bubble test — do this today 1. Hold the sealed float under water 2. Watch for 30 seconds 3. Any stream of bubbles = reject it
What a slow leak costs A 210 ℓ drum that takes on water at 1 ℓ per minute loses 30 kg of lift in half an hour — quietly, while you're in the middle of the dam.

Non-NegotiableSafety on the build site

While building

  • Eye protection when cutting or drilling
  • Gloves for rope work and sharp edges
  • Closed shoes — always
  • Tools returned to the bench, never left on the deck
  • Lift with four people, not two heroes

Before next week's water test

  • Life jacket for every person on or near the water
  • Two adult supervisors, one in the water
  • Throw line and reach pole on the bank
  • Nobody tests alone, ever
  • Test in shallow water first
The rule that overrides every other rule If it doesn't feel safe, stop and say so. Nobody is ever in trouble for stopping.
