1 Using this guide

This manual covers everything in the three-week raft course. You do not need to read it front to back — find the section you need, take the formula, and check your method against the worked example beside it.

Every worked example uses realistic materials and South African prices, and every number in it has been checked. The practice problems in Section 11 have full worked answers you can open once you've tried them.

Three habits that will carry you through the whole course Work in metres. Show every step. Round lift down and weight up.

Why those three?

  • Work in metres. Mixing centimetres and metres in one calculation is the single most common way to be wrong by a factor of a hundred. Convert everything first, then calculate.
  • Show every step. Marks come from method. A wrong final number with clear working scores far better than a right number that appeared from nowhere.
  • Round lift down, weight up. When you're unsure, round in the direction that makes your raft safer. Then any error you've made works in your favour.

2 Archimedes' Principle and buoyancy

This is the one idea the whole course rests on. Everything else is arithmetic applied to it.

Archimedes' Principle An object in a fluid is pushed upward by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it pushes out of the way.

Put something in water and the water must move aside to make room. The weight of that displaced water is the upward force you get. A shape that shoves aside 200 kg of water gets 200 kg of lift — whatever that shape is made of.

Buoyant force — the full version Fb = ρ × V × g ρ = density of the fluid, kg/m³ (fresh water = 1000) V = volume of fluid displaced, m³ g = gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s² Fb comes out in newtons (N)

The shortcut you'll actually use

Because one litre of fresh water weighs exactly one kilogram, you can skip newtons entirely for design work:

The one-litre rule 1 litre submerged  =  1 kg of lift Fresh water only. Sea water gives 1.025 kg per litre.
Three states

Floats — buoyancy exceeds weight; it rises until the submerged part displaces exactly its own weight. Sinks — buoyancy is less than weight even fully submerged. Neutral — exactly equal, so it hovers at any depth. That's how a submarine works.

The mistake everyone makes once

A float only gives you its full lift when it is completely under water. A drum floating half out is giving you half its rated lift. Quote full lift only when you've checked the thing is actually submerged at your design load.

3 Density — will it float on its own?

Density ρ = m ÷ V m = mass in kg   V = volume in m³   ρ in kg/m³ Divide by 1000 to get kg per litre.

Compare a material's density to water's. Less dense than 1000 kg/m³ and it floats by itself. Denser and it sinks — unless you seal air inside it, which is exactly what a sealed pipe or a closed drum does.

4 Volume of the shapes you'll use

Volume is step one of every buoyancy calculation. Measure in metres, calculate in cubic metres, then multiply by 1000 for litres.

ShapeFormulaYou'll use it for
Rectangular prismV = l × w × hFoam blocks, timber, deck sections
CylinderV = π r² hDrums, sealed pipe, jerry cans
Hollow tube (wall only)V = π (R² − r²) hWorking out what a pipe weighs
SphereV = ⁴⁄₃ π r³Buoys, inflatable balls
Triangular prismV = ½ b h × LShaped bows, A-frame floats
Two volumes, two jobs — don't mix them up

For a sealed pipe you need both. The outer volume is what displaces water, so it gives you lift. The wall volume is what the plastic actually occupies, so it gives you the pipe's mass. Use the outer for buoyancy, the wall for weight.

Worked example 4.1 — a sealed pipe, both volumes

A 3 m length of PVC pipe has an outside diameter of 110 mm and an inside diameter of 101.6 mm. Both ends are sealed. Find its lift and its mass.

R = 0.110 ÷ 2 = 0.055 m   r = 0.1016 ÷ 2 = 0.0508 m Outer V = π × 0.055² × 3 = 0.02851 m³ = 28.5 ℓ Inner V = π × 0.0508² × 3 = 0.02432 m³ Wall V = 0.02851 − 0.02432 = 0.00419 m³ Mass = 0.00419 × 1400 = 5.9 kg Lift = 28.5 ℓ × 1.0 = 28.5 kg Net lift = 28.5 − 5.9 = 22.6 kg per pipe

5 Net lift and the safety factor

Your raft must float itself before it floats anyone. Frame, rope, decking and fixings all come off the top.

The equation that actually matters Net lift = Total displacement − Raft's own weight
Safe working load Safe load = Net lift ÷ 1.5 And running it backwards to check a real load: Actual safety factor = Net lift ÷ Load carried

Why 1.5, and why it isn't optional

Your calculation assumes every measurement is exact, every seal holds, nobody is heavier than assumed and no timber absorbs water. None of that survives contact with a dam. The safety factor is the margin that absorbs the difference.

StructureTypical factorWhy
Passenger lift cables8–12×Total failure kills; weight penalty is cheap
Road bridges~2×Loads well understood, inspected regularly
Aircraft structure1.5×Every kilogram costs fuel — so every part is tested and tracked
This course's rafts1.5× minimumRough materials, but shallow water and life jackets
Worked example 5.1 — a complete raft

A raft uses 8 foam blocks of 150 ℓ each (foam at 32 kg/m³) and 12 sealed PVC pipes displacing 24.3 ℓ each and weighing 8.0 kg each. The frame weighs 25 kg and the rope and fixings 12 kg. Find the safe working load.

FOAM disp = 8 × 150 = 1 200 ℓ mass = 1 200 × 0.032 = 38.4 kg PIPES disp = 12 × 24.3 = 292 ℓ mass = 12 × 8.0 = 96 kg FRAME mass = 25 kg FIXING mass = 12 kg Total displacement = 1 492 kg Total own weight = 171 kg Net lift = 1 492 − 171 = 1 321 kg Safe load = 1 321 ÷ 1.5 = 880 kg

Four scouts at 80 kg is 320 kg, so the actual safety factor is 1 321 ÷ 320 = 4.1× — comfortably inside the requirement.

Notice what the frame did

Frame and fixings displace nothing useful but weigh 37 kg. Every kilogram of structure is a kilogram of crew you can't carry. Build it strong enough and no stronger.

6 Centre of gravity and stability

Total weight tells you whether it floats. Where that weight sits tells you how it floats — level, bow-down, or upside down.

Centre of gravity — taking moments CoG = Σ(m × x) ÷ Σm m = each mass   x = its distance from one chosen end Measure every x from the same end. Σ means "add up all of them".
Worked example 6.1 — the same raft, loaded two ways

A 3 m raft weighs 171 kg, centred at 1.5 m from the stern. Four scouts of 80 kg board it. Compare them crowded at the bow against spread evenly.

Crowded at the bow — at 2.2, 2.4, 2.6, 2.8 m Σ(m×x) = 171×1.5 + 80×(2.2+2.4+2.6+2.8) = 256.5 + 800 = 1 056.5 Σm = 171 + 320 = 491 kg CoG = 1 056.5 ÷ 491 = 2.15 m → 0.65 m forward of centre
Spread evenly — at 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.4 m Σ(m×x) = 256.5 + 80×(0.6+1.2+1.8+2.4) = 256.5 + 480 = 736.5 CoG = 736.5 ÷ 491 = 1.50 m → dead centre

Identical raft, identical total weight, identical buoyancy. Only the positions changed — and the first one puts the bow under water.

Why width beats everything

Tip a raft and its centre of buoyancy shifts sideways toward the submerged side. On a wide raft it shifts far enough to create a righting moment that pushes the raft back level. On a narrow one it doesn't, and the tip keeps going.

Three words to design by Low. Wide. Even.
  • Low — heavy things near the deck; crew sit or kneel, never stand.
  • Wide — buoyancy at the outer edges, not bunched in the middle.
  • Even — balanced front to back and side to side. Check both.

7 Draft and freeboard

Draft is how deep the raft sits. Freeboard is how much stays above the water. Freeboard is what keeps waves out, so it's the number that decides whether a ride is safe or wet.

Draft, for a raft with vertical sides Draft (m) = Total mass (kg) ÷ 1000 ÷ Waterplane area (m²) Waterplane area = the footprint the raft presents to the water surface. Multiply the result by 100 for centimetres.
Freeboard Freeboard = Float height − Draft Keep at least 10 cm at full design load.
Worked example 7.1

A 3.0 m × 2.0 m raft weighs 171 kg and carries four scouts at 80 kg. Its floats stand 30 cm tall. Find the draft and check the freeboard.

Total mass = 171 + 320 = 491 kg Water displaced = 491 ℓ = 0.491 m³ Waterplane area = 3.0 × 2.0 = 6.0 m² Draft = 0.491 ÷ 6.0 = 0.082 m Draft = 8.2 cm Freeboard = 30 − 8.2 = 21.8 cm ✓ well over the 10 cm minimum
When the straight-line rule stops working

This formula assumes the sides are vertical, so each extra centimetre of draft displaces the same amount. True for foam blocks and rectangular floats. For a round drum past its halfway point, less new area meets the water each centimetre, so the draft starts increasing faster than the load does. Beyond half-submerged drums, expect to sink quicker than the maths says.

8 Drag in water

Drag is the force the water pushes back with when you try to move through it. It decides how hard your crew has to paddle.

Drag force Fd = ½ × ρ × v² × Cd × A ρ = water density, 1000 kg/m³ v = speed through the water, m/s Cₒ = drag coefficient — blunt raft ≈ 1.2, shaped bow ≈ 0.8 A = frontal area under water, m² (width × draft) Fₒ comes out in newtons. Divide by 9.81 for kgf.
The part that surprises everyone Drag depends on speed squared. Double the speed and you need four times the force.
Worked example 8.1

A raft is 2.0 m wide with a draft of 8 cm and a blunt front (Cd = 1.2). Find the drag at 1 m/s and at 2 m/s.

A = 2.0 × 0.08 = 0.16 m² At 1 m/s: F = 0.5 × 1000 × 1² × 1.2 × 0.16 F = 96 N ≈ 9.8 kgf At 2 m/s: F = 0.5 × 1000 × 2² × 1.2 × 0.16 F = 384 N ≈ 39 kgf — four times as much

What you can actually change

  • A — frontal area. A narrower raft, or one that floats higher, pushes less water aside. This is the biggest lever you have.
  • Cd — shape. A pointed or angled bow instead of a flat wall can cut the coefficient by a third.
  • v — speed. Backwards, but real: easing off slightly saves far more effort than it costs in time.
  • ρ — density. You can't change the water. Stop trying.
The trade-off this creates

Everything that makes a raft stable — wide, flat, plenty of buoyancy — also makes it slow. Everything that makes it fast makes it tippy. There is no raft that wins both. Pick which one your water actually needs.

9 Measurement, error and accuracy

No measurement is exact. Engineering isn't about avoiding error — it's about knowing how big yours is and where it came from.

Percentage error % error = (predicted − measured) ÷ measured × 100 Keep the sign. Negative means you predicted less than really happened.

Two kinds of error

 SystematicRandom
Looks likeWrong by the same amount every timeScattered above and below
Caused byUnaccounted mass, mis-set scale, always reading from the same wrong angleRipples, eye position, someone shifting weight
FixFind the cause and correct itTake more readings and average
WarningAveraging will not helpAveraging is exactly the right move

Using buoyancy backwards as a measuring instrument

If your raft sits deeper than predicted, you can work out exactly how much extra mass is aboard — without weighing anything. This is Archimedes' Principle running in reverse, and it's the most powerful trick in the course.

Worked example 9.1 — finding mass you never weighed

A patrol predicted a draft of 8.6 cm at their design load of 518 kg total. They measured 10.05 cm. Their raft's waterplane area is 6.0 m². What went wrong, and by how much?

% error = (8.6 − 10.05) ÷ 10.05 × 100 = −14.4% Volume displaced = 0.1005 × 6.0 = 0.603 m³ = 603 ℓ → 603 kg actually afloat Predicted = 518 kg Unaccounted mass = 603 − 518 = 85 kg

Now account for the 85 kg with named causes: crew heavier than the assumed 80 kg (+40), water inside a leaking float (+25), timber that absorbed water (+15), wet rope (+5). The numbers add up, and that is a finished error analysis.

"Human error" is not an explanation

It's a way of avoiding one. Every error you report needs a named cause and an estimated size. "We read the stick from standing, which made every draft look about 2 cm shallow" is analysis. "There was some human error" is not.

11 Practice problems

Try each one on paper first, then open the answer. Full working is shown — compare your method, not just your final number.

Problem 1 · Volume

A foam block measures 1.2 m × 0.6 m × 0.25 m. Find its volume in cubic metres and in litres.

Show answer
V = 1.2 × 0.6 × 0.25 = 0.18 m³ V = 0.18 × 1000 = 180 litres
Problem 2 · Volume of a cylinder

A sealed drum is 0.58 m in diameter and 0.88 m tall. Find its volume in litres.

Show answer
r = 0.58 ÷ 2 = 0.29 m V = π × 0.29² × 0.88 = π × 0.0841 × 0.88 V = 0.2325 m³ V = 233 litres
Problem 3 · Density

A block of timber has a mass of 12 kg and a volume of 14 litres. Will it float in fresh water? Show why.

Show answer
14 ℓ = 0.014 m³ ρ = 12 ÷ 0.014 = 857 kg/m³ 857 < 1000, so it floats — but only just. Once it absorbs water its density will rise toward 1000 and it will float lower, or eventually sink.
Problem 4 · Buoyant force

A 180 litre float is held completely under fresh water. What is the buoyant force, in kilograms of lift and in newtons?

Show answer
Lift = 180 ℓ × 1.0 = 180 kg F = 180 × 9.81 F = 1 766 N
Problem 5 · Net lift

The drum from Problem 2 (233 ℓ) weighs 18 kg empty and is sealed. What is its net lift?

Show answer
Displacement = 233 kg Own weight = 18 kg Net lift = 233 − 18 = 215 kg
Problem 6 · Safety factor

A raft has a net buoyancy of 540 kg. What is its safe working load at a safety factor of 1.5?

Show answer
Safe load = 540 ÷ 1.5 = 360 kg Four scouts at 80 kg = 320 kg, so this raft passes — with an actual safety factor of 540 ÷ 320 = 1.7×
Problem 7 · Complete raft — multi-step

A raft uses 6 foam blocks of 180 ℓ each (foam at 32 kg/m³) and 4 sealed drums of 232 ℓ each weighing 18 kg each. The frame and fixings together weigh 48 kg. Find the total displacement, the raft's own weight, the net lift and the safe working load.

Show answer
FOAM disp = 6 × 180 = 1 080 ℓ mass = 1 080 × 0.032 = 34.6 kg DRUMS disp = 4 × 232 = 928 ℓ mass = 4 × 18 = 72 kg FRAME mass = 48 kg Total displacement = 1 080 + 928 = 2 008 kg Total own weight = 34.6 + 72 + 48 = 154.6 kg Net lift = 2 008 − 154.6 = 1 853.4 kg Safe working load = 1 853.4 ÷ 1.5 = 1 236 kg
Problem 8 · Draft

The raft from Problem 7 measures 3.2 m × 1.8 m. It carries its own weight plus five scouts averaging 78 kg. How deep does it sit?

Show answer
Crew = 5 × 78 = 390 kg Total mass = 154.6 + 390 = 544.6 kg Volume displaced = 0.5446 m³ Waterplane area = 3.2 × 1.8 = 5.76 m² Draft = 0.5446 ÷ 5.76 = 0.0946 m Draft = 9.5 cm
Problem 9 · Freeboard

The floats on that raft stand 30 cm tall. Does it meet the 10 cm minimum freeboard rule?

Show answer
Freeboard = 30 − 9.5 Freeboard = 20.5 cm ✓ passes comfortably
Problem 10 · Centre of gravity

The raft's own 154.6 kg acts at 1.6 m from the stern. The five scouts sit at 0.5, 1.1, 1.7, 2.3 and 2.9 m. Find the centre of gravity and say whether the raft is balanced.

Show answer
Σ(m×x) = 154.6 × 1.6 + 78 × (0.5+1.1+1.7+2.3+2.9) = 247.4 + 78 × 8.5 = 247.4 + 663 = 910.4 Σm = 154.6 + 390 = 544.6 kg CoG = 910.4 ÷ 544.6 = 1.67 m The raft's centre is at 1.6 m, so the CoG is 7 cm forward of centre — slightly bow-heavy, but acceptable.
Problem 11 · Drag

That raft is 1.8 m wide with a draft of 9.5 cm and a blunt bow (Cd = 1.2). Find the drag at 0.8 m/s and at 1.6 m/s, and state the ratio between them.

Show answer
A = 1.8 × 0.095 = 0.171 m² At 0.8 m/s: F = 0.5 × 1000 × 0.64 × 1.2 × 0.171 F = 66 N At 1.6 m/s: F = 0.5 × 1000 × 2.56 × 1.2 × 0.171 F = 263 N Ratio = 4×. Speed doubled, drag quadrupled — as v² requires.
Problem 12 · Error analysis — hardest

The patrol predicted a draft of 9.5 cm but measured 11.2 cm. Their waterplane area is 5.76 m² and their predicted total mass was 544.6 kg. Find the percentage error and the unaccounted mass, then suggest where it came from.

Show answer
% error = (9.5 − 11.2) ÷ 11.2 × 100 = −15.2% (predicted too shallow) Volume displaced = 0.112 × 5.76 = 0.645 m³ = 645 ℓ → 645 kg afloat Predicted = 544.6 kg Unaccounted mass = 645 − 544.6 = 100 kg

Likely sources, which should add to roughly 100 kg: scouts heavier than the assumed 78 kg average, water inside a leaking float, timber and rope that absorbed water, and any gear left on the deck during the test. To confirm, weigh each scout, lift each float out and shake it, and weigh a dry offcut against a soaked one.
