Every person, every time

  • Life jacket on and fastened before you approach the edge
  • Shallow water first — waist deep, no exceptions
  • Two supervisors on duty, one in the water
  • Throw line and reach pole on the bank, in someone's hands

If something goes wrong

  • Let the raft go — it floats, you swim
  • Shout, don't struggle silently
  • Reach or throw from the bank. Never swim to a rescue
  • Head count after every single test run
Overrides everything Any person may stop a test at any moment, for any reason, and does not have to explain why.

Part One

The test protocol

Week 3 · Testing & Analysis
04
Three phases, in order, no skipping. A patrol that jumps to maximum load has no baseline and no usable data.

Phase OneUnloaded — establish your baseline

Float the raft empty. Nobody aboard, nothing on the deck.

This tells you the truth about your raft's own weight — including every litre of water the timber and rope have already absorbed since you built it.

Every later measurement is compared against this one, so take your time and measure it properly.

Record Draft at each of the 4 corners Does it sit level?  Y / N Which corner sits lowest? Any bubbles rising?  Y / N Average unloaded draft = ____ cm
Four corners, not oneIf the four readings differ by more than 2 cm, your raft is out of balance — that is a finding, and it belongs in your analysis. Don't fix it silently.
Week 3 · Testing & Analysis
05
Slow them down here. The temptation is to rush to loading. A sloppy baseline poisons every number that follows.

Phase TwoProgressive loading — one step at a time

  1. Add one known weight — a scout, or a measured container of water
  2. Let the raft settle completely
  3. Measure the draft at all four corners
  4. Record it before adding anything else
  5. Repeat until you reach your design load
Known weights onlyWeigh each scout on the scale before they board. "About 70 kg" is not data.
Load evenlyPlace each new weight where your Week 2 layout said it goes. Changing the plan mid-test makes the results meaningless.

06
One variable at a time is the core experimental discipline of the whole course. Name it as such — it's the reason their data will be worth analysing.

Phase ThreeMaximum load — and when to stop

Keep adding weight past the design load, carefully, until one of these happens.

Stop immediately if · Freeboard drops below 5 cm · Water comes over any edge · Any joint creaks, moves or shifts · The raft starts to list · Anyone says stop
We are not testing to destruction The answer you want is the last load that was clearly safe.

Real engineers test prototypes to failure in a laboratory — never with people aboard.

Record the momentWrite down the load, the draft, and which condition made you stop. That last detail is worth marks.

07
Supervisors call the stop, not the patrol. Watch freeboard continuously during this phase. Use water containers rather than extra scouts beyond the design load.

TechniqueHow to measure draft accurately

Use a marked stickMark a straight stick in centimetres. Hold it vertically against the raft's edge and read where the waterline crosses it.
Eye level with the waterReading from above makes the draft look smaller than it is. Crouch down.
Wait for stillnessLet the ripples settle. If it's windy, take three readings and average them.
Week 3 · Testing & Analysis
08
Parallax is the big one. Demonstrate it — read a stick from standing, then from crouching. The difference is often 2 cm, which is larger than most of the effects they're trying to detect.

Pause here. Ask what they notice before advancing. Someone will spot that the difference never changes. Let them say it — the next three slides build on it.

Quantify ItPercentage error

% error  =  (predicted − measured) ÷ measured × 100
At the design load Predicted = 8.6 cm Measured  = 10.1 cm (8.6 − 10.1) ÷ 10.1 × 100 = −14.1%
Read the signA negative result means you predicted less than really happened. The raft sat 14% deeper than the maths said it would.
Is 14% bad?For a first prototype built by hand from rough materials, 14% is a good result. What matters is whether you can explain it.

11
Insist on the sign. "14% out" is a weaker answer than "14% under-predicted". Direction tells you where to look for the cause.

The Clever BitWorking backwards to find the missing mass

Turn the measured draft back into kilograms Measured draft   = 10.05 cm = 0.1005 m Waterplane area = 6.0 m² Volume displaced = 0.1005 × 6.0 = 0.603 m³                  = 603 ℓ → 603 kg afloat ───────────────────────────── We predicted    = 518 kg Unaccounted mass = 85 kg
What just happened One measurement with a stick told you there were 85 unexplained kilograms on your raft.

You never weighed it. You worked it out from how deep it sat — which is Archimedes' Principle, running in reverse.

Have them do this for their own raft. They must list causes and estimate magnitudes. A list without numbers scores about half marks in Section 3 of the homework.

Two Kinds of ErrorSystematic or random?

Systematic Wrong by the same amount, every time.

Our +1.4 cm on every row. Caused by something constant — unaccounted mass, a mis-set scale, always reading the stick from above.

Fix: find the cause and correct it. Averaging more readings will not help at all.

Random Scattered — sometimes high, sometimes low.

Ripples on the water, a slightly different eye position, someone shifting their weight as you read.

Fix: take more readings and average. The scatter shrinks; the true value emerges.

How to tell which you haveLook down the difference column. Constant → systematic. Jumping around zero → random. Ours was dead constant, which is why we went hunting for hidden mass rather than re-measuring.

Genuine science-fair-grade content. If they take one transferable idea from this course into school physics, make it this one.

The RelationshipIs draft proportional to weight?

Both lines are straight and parallel. Draft really is proportional to load — 1.33 cm for every 80 kg, exactly as the maths predicted.

So the physics was rightThe slope matched perfectly. Only the starting point was wrong — which is precisely what a systematic error looks like on a graph.
When it stops being straightOnce a round drum passes its halfway point, less new area meets the water each centimetre — so draft starts climbing faster. Load past that and the line bends upward, sharply.

StandardWhat good analysis looks like

Strong

  • "We predicted 8.6 cm and measured 10.1 cm — 14% under."
  • "The gap was constant, so the cause is systematic."
  • "Working back, 85 kg was unaccounted for."
  • "Weighing the crew found 40 kg of it; a wet float held 25 kg more."
  • "Next time we weigh the crew and bubble-test on the day."

Weak

  • "It floated fine."
  • "Our calculations were a bit off."
  • "There was probably some human error."
  • "The raft was heavier than we thought."
  • "Next time we'd build it better."
The difference Every strong statement carries a number and a cause. "Human error" on its own is not an explanation — it's a way of not giving one.
