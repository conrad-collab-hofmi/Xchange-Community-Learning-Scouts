The QuestionA steel bolt sinks. A steel ship doesn't.

A 5 gram steel bolt drops straight to the bottom.

A container ship made of the same steel weighs 200 000 tonnes and stays on the surface.

Same material. Opposite result.

So floating cannot be about what a thing is made of. It has to be about something else.

Hold this thought It's not the material.
It's the shape.

A bolt is solid steel. A ship is mostly air, wrapped in a thin steel skin. The ship pushes far more water out of the way — and that is the whole secret.

The Core PrincipleArchimedes' Principle

Stated once, used all term Any object in a fluid is pushed upward by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it pushes out of the way.

Put a block in water and the water has to go somewhere. The weight of that displaced water is the upward force you get back. Nothing more, nothing less.

Push a beach ball under and you feel this fighting you. Let go and it fires out — because it displaced a lot of water for very little weight of its own.

The formula Fb = ρ × V × g
ρ  = density of water (kg/m³) V  = volume pushed aside (m³) g  = gravity, 9.81 m/s²

05
3 min. Stress that a floating object settles at the depth where the two forces are equal. It doesn't keep rising out of the water — it stops when balance is reached. That depth is the draft, slide 14.

The ShortcutThe one-litre rule

1 litre of volume under water  =  1 kilogram of lift

Fresh water has a density of 1000 kg/m³. One cubic metre holds 1000 litres, so one litre of fresh water weighs exactly one kilogram.

That means you can skip the full formula for every rough calculation this term. Work out the volume in litres, and you have the lift in kilograms.

Sea water is different Sea water is denser — about 1025 kg/m³ — so every litre gives you 1.025 kg of lift. That's why a boat sits slightly higher at sea than in a river. For our dam testing, use 1.0.

Worked Example 1The sealed 210 ℓ drum

A sealed plastic drum holds 210 litres and weighs 15 kg empty. Will it float, and how much can it hold up?

Working Volume    = 210 ℓ Lift        = 210 × 1.0 = 210 kg Own weight = 15 kg Net lift   = 210 − 15 = 195 kg
Answer Yes — and it carries 195 kg

Roughly two and a half scouts, from a single drum. This is why drums are the workhorse of raft building.

The catchThat 210 kg only appears when the drum is fully submerged. A drum floating half out of the water is only giving you half its lift.

Worked Example 2A foam block, all the way to a safe load

Closed-cell foam · 2.0 m × 0.8 m × 0.4 m · 32 kg/m³ 1. V = 2.0 × 0.8 × 0.4 = 0.64 m³ 2. In litres = 0.64 × 1000 = 640 ℓ 3. Lift = 640 × 1.0 = 640 kg 4. Own weight = 640 × 0.032 = 20.5 kg 5. Net lift = 640 − 20.5 = 619.5 kg 6. Safe load = 619.5 ÷ 1.5 = 413 kg

The lesson PVC eats a third of its own lift

Foam gave away 3% to its own weight. PVC gives away 33%. Six sealed pipes won't carry one scout safely.

So why use it?Pipe makes an excellent frame — stiff, cheap, waterproof. Use it for structure, and get your lift from foam or drums.

The Equation That MattersNet buoyancy

Lift you can use  =  Total displacement  −  the raft's own weight

Engineering PracticeWhy we divide by 1.5

Your calculation assumes everything is perfect. Reality is not.

  • Timber absorbs water and gains weight
  • A seal leaks and a pipe half fills
  • Everyone crowds to one side at once
  • A scout is heavier than the 70 kg you assumed
  • Your tape measure was off by 3 cm

A safety factor is the margin that absorbs all of it. 1.5× is our minimum for anything carrying people.

Both directions Safe load = Net lift ÷ 1.5 Actual SF = Net lift ÷ Load carried
Example Net lift = 200 kg Safe load = 200 ÷ 1.5 = 133 kg
For comparisonLifts use 8–12×. Bridges use about 2×. Aircraft use 1.5× — and weigh every bolt to keep it there.

How It SitsDraft and freeboard

Draft is what's under. Freeboard is what's left above.
3 m × 2 m raft, 491 kg loaded Water to displace = 491 ℓ = 0.491 m³ Waterplane area = 3 × 2 = 6 m² Draft = 0.491 ÷ 6 = 0.082 m = 8.2 cm Freeboard = 30 − 8.2 = 21.8 cm
Design ruleKeep at least 10 cm of freeboard when fully loaded. Less than that and the first wave comes aboard.

Moving Through WaterDrag doubles badly

Drag force Fd = ½ × ρ × v² × Cd × A
v = speed   Cd = shape factor   A = area facing forward

The v² is the whole story. Double your speed and drag doesn't double — it quadruples.

2 m wide raft, 8 cm draft, Cₒ = 1.2
What you controlYou can't change the water. You can change A — how much raft is shoved through the water — and Cd, by pointing a shaped nose forward instead of a flat wall.

Staying UprightWhy wide wins

● centre of gravity    ● centre of buoyancy

Tip a raft and its centre of buoyancy shifts sideways toward the submerged side. On a wide raft it shifts far enough to push the raft back level. On a narrow one it doesn't — and over you go.

Three rulesBuild wide. Keep mass low — sit, don't stand. Spread load evenly side to side.

How Engineers WorkThe design process

  1. Define the problem and the constraints
  2. Research materials and what others have done
  3. Specify requirements — load, budget, size
  4. Generate several different solutions
  1. Choose one and justify why
  2. Build a prototype
  3. Test it and record real data
  4. Improve — then go back to step 4
It's a loop, not a lineStep 8 sends you back to step 4. Real engineering teams go round this circle many times. Over three weeks you go round it once — Week 1 is steps 1–5, Week 2 is step 6, Week 3 is steps 7 and 8.
